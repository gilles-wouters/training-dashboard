    §3  Am I getting fitter?

    Aerobic efficiency · metres per heartbeat

    Each easy run 5-run average

    How far each heartbeat carries you. It strips out weather, terrain and mood and asks one question: at a given cardiac cost, how much distance do you get? Watch the orange line, not the dots. Rising over six to eight weeks means the training is working. Flat for two months at consistent volume means something needs to change: more volume, more intensity, or more sleep. Falling while volume is steady usually means fatigue or heat rather than lost fitness.

    Heart rate against pace · every run

    Older Recent

    The single most honest picture of fitness change. Each dot is one run: pace across, heart rate up. You want the blue dots sitting below and to the left of the grey ones — same pace at a lower heart rate, or faster at the same heart rate. If the blue cloud sits directly on top of the grey, you have held fitness but not built it.

    Aerobic durability · decoupling on long runs

    How much your heart rate drifts upward through a long run relative to pace. Under 5% means the aerobic base held all the way. Above 10% means you outran it and the second half cost far more than the first. Falling over a block is the signature of genuine endurance being built, and it usually improves before race pace does.

    §4  Am I training consistently?

    Weekly volume · against your own rolling average

    Week 4-week average

    No fixed target, because a target set in August is wrong by October. The orange line is your own four-week average, so the question becomes whether you are trending up, holding, or sliding. Sustainable building looks like three weeks up, one week down, repeating.

    §5  Cadence and stride

    A note on targets, because this gets misreported constantly. There is no universal optimal cadence — the famous 180 figure came from counting elite 5k runners at racing speed and does not generalise. Height and leg length set your natural range, and at 187 cm yours sits legitimately lower than a 170 cm runner's. Weight barely enters into it. What the evidence does support is that nudging your own habitual cadence up by 5 to 10% shortens your stride slightly, reduces braking force, and cuts load at the knee and hip. So the number to chase is your own baseline plus 5%, not somebody else's 180.

    Cadence against pace · every run

    Older Recent Reference band for 187 cm

    Cadence only means anything relative to speed — it should rise naturally as you run faster, so a single number across all your runs hides more than it shows. The shape you want is a clear upward slope from left to right. Flat means you are lengthening your stride to go faster instead of turning over quicker, which is the pattern most associated with overstriding and heel braking. The green band is a height-scaled guide, not a rule; sitting just below it while sloping correctly is fine.

    Cadence over time · easy runs only

    Each run 5-run average Your baseline +5%

    Easy runs only, so pace variation does not muddy the trend. The dashed line is 5% above your own average from the last three months — the target the injury literature actually supports. Move toward it over months, not weeks. A sudden 10% jump feels awful, raises your heart rate at the same pace, and usually gets abandoned. Two or three steps per minute a month sticks. Practise it during strides, where it is easiest, and let it bleed into your easy running.

    Stride length against pace

    Each run Reference band

    Stride length is not independent of cadence: at a given speed the two multiply to a constant, so shortening one lengthens the other. Expressed against your height, stride runs from roughly 0.52 of your height at easy pace to about 0.72 at 5k pace. Sitting well above the band at easy pace is the clearest sign of overstriding, and it is also the cheapest thing on this page to fix — think about landing under your hips rather than reaching out in front, and let the cadence do the work.

    §6  Am I recovering?

    Resting HR and HRV · 120 days

    Resting HR HRV

    Ignore the daily wobble entirely at this scale. Resting HR drifting down and HRV drifting up is what a year of good adaptation looks like. The two crossing the wrong way for several weeks while training load rises is the earliest warning of overreaching you will get.

    Fitness, fatigue and form

    Fitness Fatigue Form

    Form is fitness minus fatigue. Deeply negative form during a build is correct and expected. You want it climbing back toward zero in the ten days before anything you care about racing. Chronically negative for months without a recovery week is how people arrive at December injured rather than sharp.

    Sleep · last 8 weeks

    The largest modifiable lever you have, and the one most likely to explain a bad block. The line is your seven-night average; that is the number that matters, not any single night. If it sits under seven hours for a fortnight, no amount of training design compensates.
